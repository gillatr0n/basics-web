What is Dungeons & Dragons?

D&D stands for Dungeons & Dragons. It's a cooperative tabletop roleplaying game. You and the other players create fantasy characters and decide what they do in an imaginary world.

One person, called the Dungeon Master (DM), describes and runs that world. Your decisions shape the adventure, while rules and dice help resolve uncertain outcomes. The point is to enjoy a shared story together, rather than defeat the other players.

How does a game work?

A session is one meeting to play. It usually follows this pattern:

  1. The Dungeon Master describes the situation.
  2. The players decide what their characters attempt.
  3. The Dungeon Master explains what happens, using rules or dice when needed.

A locked door in an old castle

Your group reaches a locked door. One player tries to pick the lock, another searches for a different entrance, and someone suggests knocking.

The DM might describe an answer to the knock or a side entrance found during the search. An uncertain attempt, such as picking the lock, may need a roll. The result gives the group its next decision.

What do you do during an adventure?

An adventure is the story or situation your group is playing through. It can involve several different kinds of activity.

Exploration

You discover places, search for clues, solve puzzles, and investigate mysteries. You might look for a hidden passage or work out what a strange inscription means.

Social interaction

You talk to characters played by the DM, make friends, and negotiate. You might ask a villager about the castle or persuade a guard to let your group inside.

Combat

Characters and enemies take turns during a fight, using abilities and dice to resolve their actions. Your group might defend itself from monsters in a cellar.

An adventure can last one session or continue over several. A longer series of connected adventures with the same group of characters is called a campaign.

Characters

A character is someone you create and control in the game, with their own personality, goals, and abilities.

A class influences those abilities, but not their whole personality. These are three examples; many other classes exist.

Three example classes
Class What they're good at Example
Fighter Weapons, armor, and combat Protecting the group during a fight
Rogue Stealth, skills, and precise attacks Sneaking past guards or picking a lock
Wizard Learning and casting spells Using magic to solve problems

How dice work

Players roll dice when an action could succeed or fail and the outcome matters. The roll helps the group find out what happens, such as whether your character spots a hidden entrance.

A D20 is a die with twenty sides. You often add a number from your character sheet to the roll. This adjustment is called a modifier and can be a bonus or a penalty.

Suppose the DM sets a target number of 15 to find a hidden entrance:

12 on the die + 3 bonus = 15 total

Meeting or beating the target succeeds, so you find the entrance. A lower total would fail. Not every action needs a roll; the DM uses the rules to decide when dice are needed.

Anyone can play D&D

You don't need to memorize the rules, act in character voices, or own expensive equipment. You can learn as you play. A first session needs just a few practical things:

  • People to play with: someone to run the game as the DM, and other players to join you.
  • A character: ask for a premade one if you'd like to try playing before creating your own.
  • Dice or a digital dice roller: sharing a set of dice is fine.
  • A way to keep track: a paper or digital character sheet records your character's abilities and belongings. A pencil or notes app helps too.

Ask the person running the game what to bring and how long the session will last.
